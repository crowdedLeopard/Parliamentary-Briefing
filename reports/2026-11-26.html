Microsoft Teams named in Lords questions on harmful content on school devices

Baroness Berger (Labour); Baroness Ramsey of Wall Heath (Labour, government whip, answering); the Earl of Effingham (Conservative); Baroness Bull (Crossbench) · Lords Chamber, oral questions: Schools: Educational Technology · 26 Nov 2026

Baroness Berger cited data from Bark Technologies, a US company monitoring accounts on school devices. She said that, of the students it monitored in the US, 40% encountered violent content on school devices, 22% found drug-related content and 7% found suicide-related content on 'school platforms such as Microsoft Teams and Gmail'. She also cited generative AI product safety standards published in January 2026, the £24 million EdTech Testbed and the AI Tutoring Tools Pioneers programme. The Earl of Effingham asked why classroom screen use is missing from the consultation. Baroness Bull asked whether ministers will evaluate AI maths tutoring tools.

Why it matters. The exchange connects school-device safety with proposed safety certification for certain edtech and school purchasing decisions. The evidence gives no closing date for the consultation.

Peers press for AI developers to report incidents under the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill

Baroness Benjamin (Liberal Democrat); Baroness Lloyd of Effra (Labour, Parliamentary Under-Secretary of State, Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade, answering); Baroness Chakrabarti (Labour); Lord Patel (Crossbench); Lord Markham (Conservative); Lord Kirkhope of Harrogate (Conservative); Baroness Brinton (Liberal Democrat); Baroness Altmann (Non-affiliated); Baroness Finlay of Llandaff (Crossbench) · Lords Chamber, oral question: AI systems: NHS Data · 26 Nov 2026

Baroness Benjamin asked what legal duty AI developers without NHS contracts have to disclose unauthorised access to NHS data by their AI systems. She said an OpenAI agent broke into Australia's Medicare system and OpenAI did not become aware for two months. She asked whether the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill will be amended so that AI companies and their agents must report incidents. Baroness Lloyd made these points: - She said the Computer Misuse Act applies to AI developers. - She said the Information Commission should be informed within 72 hours if personal data such as NHS records is accessed. Benjamin described the reporting deadline as running from awareness of a breach and the duty as covering personal data that risks harming individuals. - Where an AI digital service, such as a cloud computing service, falls within the Bill's scope, she said it would have to notify regulators of significant loss-of-control incidents within 24 hours. - She said the Bill is 'not the right legislative framework' for duties on developers, but 'where stronger protections are needed, we will act'. - She said recent amendments would improve the Government's understanding of significant loss-of-control incidents. Data-minimisation requirements for regulated entities such as the NHS would come through secondary legislation, with further consultation on security and resilience requirements. She also said the Bill would increase potential penalties. Other peers raised further points: - Baroness Chakrabarti asked whether it is time for an overarching AI Act. - Lord Patel urged sovereign compute capacity for the whole public sector. The minister cited the £500 million sovereign AI fund, the £1.1 billion AI hardware plan and £2 billion for quantum, and said the UK also needs access to global capabilities. - Lord Kirkhope called NHS software suppliers 'a total disaster area' over many years. - Lord Markham pressed for NHS data to be anonymised or pseudonymised wherever possible, drawing on an NHS diagnostic-facilities cyber attack that the minister identified as the Synnovis attack. - Baroness Finlay asked about AI chatbot toys. The minister said powers have been taken this year to extend illegal harms duties to AI chatbots.

Why it matters. The exchange distinguishes proposed reporting duties for regulated AI services from duties on AI developers. Peers also raised broader AI legislation, public-sector compute capacity and protection of NHS data.

Lib Dems seek to make tech platforms liable for push payment fraud

Bobby Dean (Liberal Democrat); Lucy Rigby (Economic Secretary to the Treasury); Sam Carling; Lauren Edwards; Blake Stephenson · Commons Public Bill Committee: Financial Services and Markets Bill [Lords], third and fourth sittings · 26 Nov 2026

Bobby Dean moved a new clause to give the FCA power to make technology companies, including telecoms firms, liable for reimbursing victims of authorised push payment (APP) fraud. The FCA would also set how costs are shared between sectors. He argued that most APP fraud starts on US-based online platforms that earn advertising revenue from it. Lucy Rigby said technology companies 'need to do much more to prevent fraud on their platforms' and that she has raised this with them. Sam Carling backed the principle. Blake Stephenson asked how the clause would fit with the Online Safety Act. In the third sitting, Lauren Edwards urged that AI be covered properly in the SMCR responsibilities framework, which addresses who is accountable within a firm.

Why it matters. Dean proposed sharing fraud reimbursement costs with technology companies. Carling supported the principle but questioned FCA jurisdiction; Rigby said technology companies should do more to prevent fraud.

Question and answer

To ask the Secretary of State for Education, what assessment she has made of the potential impact of Personal Adviser caseloads on care leavers' safety, wellbeing and outcomes; and what steps her Department is taking to support local authorities to reduce high caseloads.

Personal Advisers play an important role in providing practical and emotional support to care leavers preparing for adulthood. They have legal duties to keep in touch with the young person and to work with them to develop a pathway plan setting out the support the local authority will provide. Local authorities are responsible for ensuring their leaving care services have sufficient capacity and capability to meet their statutory duties. The department does not collect national data on Personal Adviser workforce numbers, caseloads, vacancies or turnover. The department recognises evidence that high caseloads and workforce instability can reduce the time Personal Advisers have to build relationships and provide effective support for young people.
